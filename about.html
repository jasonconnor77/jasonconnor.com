Connecting Departments · Aligning Priorities

Good manufacturing leadership starts with knowing what is actually happening.

I’ve spent more than 20 years in manufacturing across scheduling, purchasing, supplier management, materials, inventory, maintenance coordination, production, and operations leadership. Working in those different areas taught me that no department operates by itself. What happens in one part of the operation almost always affects another.

I’ve never been someone who looks at a problem and says, “that belongs to another department.” If something is affecting production, delivery, people, or the customer, I’m willing to get involved, understand what is happening, and help move it forward. Sometimes that means helping solve the problem directly. Other times it means bringing the right departments together, making the issue visible, and making sure everyone is working from the same priorities.

That broader experience is a big part of how I lead. A production schedule can look perfect on paper and still fail on the floor. A machine may be available, but the material is not. The material may be there, but maintenance has an issue. A supplier may be late, inventory may be wrong, or two departments may simply be working from different information. I try to understand the entire chain instead of stopping at the first explanation.

Seeing those connections shapes the way I lead today: stay close to the operation, make the real problem visible, establish clear priorities, connect the people who can affect the outcome, remove obstacles, and follow through until the result changes.

I’m comfortable stepping into difficult situations: past-due schedules, shortages, poor visibility, inventory problems, competing priorities, supplier issues, maintenance concerns, and processes that have become harder than they need to be.

When the information exists but the operation still cannot see the answer, I build a better way to surface it. That has led me to develop production, scheduling, inventory, material shortage, and management tools using Excel, Google Sheets, Google Apps Script, and ERP data. I’m not a software developer trying to understand manufacturing. I’m a manufacturing leader who learned how to build better tools for problems I was already responsible for solving.

Visibility before reaction

Problems are easier to solve when the operation can see them early and agree on what matters.

02

Priorities must be credible

A priority that ignores material, capacity, labor, or actual floor conditions is not an operating plan.

03

Accountability needs clarity

People perform better when expectations, status, ownership, and the definition of done are visible.

04

Connect the operation

The best decisions happen when scheduling, purchasing, suppliers, inventory, maintenance, production, and shipping are working from the same priorities and the same information.
